About & credits
Real data: planet positions from JPL orbital elements; the background is 1,637 naked-eye stars (HYG catalogue) in their true sky positions. Orbit spacing & body sizes are scaled for visibility — the Size & Orbits sliders morph them to true scale relative to the Sun. Rotation is tied to the date at each body's true sidereal rate & direction (Venus, Uranus & Pluto rotate retrograde), so pausing holds a fixed orientation. The terrestrial planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars) are oriented from their IAU rotational elements, so the correct longitude faces the Sun — Earth maps to real geography. The giant planets & Pluto point their axes in the true directions — Saturn's rings and Uranus's sideways roll sit in their real planes — but their absolute facing is illustrative. The dwarf planets Ceres, Haumea, Makemake & Eris use mean orbital elements, so their orbit shape & tilt are real but their position along the orbit is approximate. Moons (toggle them in Show) use real orbit shape, inclination, period & direction around their planet's true spin axis — Triton & Phoebe orbit retrograde, Saturn's moons share its ring plane, and the Moon rides the ecliptic as it really does. The major moons are placed at their real positions, calibrated for the modern era (≈1900–2100); minor moons' phase is illustrative. Zoom in on a planet to see its moons' orbit paths.
Dates & time: dates are UTC in the modern (proleptic Gregorian) calendar, even before 1582 — history books usually quote Julian-calendar dates, which differ by days to a few weeks in ancient times. The slow drift between clock time and astronomical time (ΔT) is not modelled; near the ends of the range this can shift fast-moving Mercury by a few degrees.
Comets & spacecraft: comets use fixed orbital elements, so their orbit shapes are real but positions drift from truth away from the present. Spacecraft ride JPL Horizons trajectory data (launch era – 2049, extended on their final heading beyond); markers are symbols, not to scale. Because comet and probe paths span the whole system, the compact view maps their distances through the planets' own spacing — they cross each planet's displayed orbit exactly when they cross the real one. Trails trace each body's displayed path as time runs; the Upcoming events and the tooltips' "next opposition / best view" dates are computed live from the same orbital model (typically within a day or two of almanac values).
Keyboard: Space play/pause · ← → ±1 day (Shift = month, Ctrl = year) · + − speed · 0 today · H home · F fullscreen · Esc stop following.

An interactive 3D solar system

Orrery.live is a free, interactive 3D orrery — a working model of the solar system that you explore in your web browser. Every planet is placed in its true position around the Sun using JPL orbital elements, so the view shows the real arrangement of Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune and Pluto for any date you choose. Drag to orbit the camera, scroll or pinch to zoom, right-drag (or drag with two fingers) to pan, and hover or tap any body to read live facts about it. Double-click a planet or moon to lock the camera onto it and follow it as time plays.

A time machine for the planets

Use the date picker or the time slider to scrub forwards and backwards through time, anywhere from 3000 BC to 3000 AD. Speed time up to watch the planets race around their orbits, or pause on an exact moment and copy a shareable link to it. The 'Jump to a notable alignment' menu travels straight to famous planetary alignments and conjunctions so you can see exactly how the planets lined up.

Real sky, real data

The background is the genuine night sky: 1,637 naked-eye stars from the HYG database in their true positions, with optional constellation figures and named bright stars. Planet positions come from real orbital data, while body sizes and orbit spacing are scaled up for visibility — the same compromise used by physical orreries.

Comets, spacecraft and sky events

Follow Halley's Comet, Comet Encke, Hale–Bopp and Rosetta's comet 67P on their real orbits, or trace Voyager 1 and 2, Pioneer 10 and 11 and New Horizons along trajectories from JPL Horizons. Tooltips show each planet's distance from Earth, light-travel time, where it sits in tonight's sky and its next opposition or best viewing date — and the events menu lists upcoming oppositions and elongations computed live from the orbital model.

Frequently asked questions

What is an orrery?

An orrery is a model of the solar system that shows the planets in their correct relative positions and movements around the Sun. Orrery.live is a digital, interactive 3D orrery you can explore in your browser.

How are the planet positions calculated?

Each planet's position is computed from JPL orbital elements, so the planets appear in their true heliocentric positions for any date between 3000 BC and 3000 AD. Orbit spacing and on-screen body sizes are scaled for visibility, but the angular positions are real.

How far back and forward in time can I travel?

Any date from 3000 BC to 3000 AD. Inside that range the positions are computed from the JPL data; outside it the simulation still runs but warns that positions are approximate because it is extrapolating beyond the orbital data.

Can I see planetary alignments and conjunctions?

Yes. The 'Jump to a notable alignment' menu lets you instantly travel to famous planetary alignments and conjunctions and watch how the planets line up.

What are the stars in the background?

The background is 1,637 naked-eye stars from the HYG database, placed in their true positions on the sky — the real night sky, not a decorative starfield.

Why are the planet sizes and orbits not to scale?

A true-scale solar system would leave the planets as invisible specks separated by huge empty gaps. Orrery.live keeps positions and orbital geometry real but scales body sizes and orbit spacing for visibility — and the Size and Orbits sliders let you morph the model to true scale to see the emptiness for yourself.

Is Orrery.live free, and does it work on mobile?

Orrery.live is completely free with no sign-up and runs in any modern browser that supports WebGL, including phones and tablets.
